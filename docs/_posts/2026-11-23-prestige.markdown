Now to the topic of this blog/shitpost... Prestige.
It carries a lot of weight in academia, and even industry.
So much so that a university or company's name has a disproportionately larger impact on external decisions than their actual merit.
People want to work at these places, students want to attend these schools, resources are allocated to them with a higher priority,
and research that comes out of them is taken with a smaller grain of salt.
They even wait less longer than others on arxiv moderation queues apparently.

I was once such a student. IITs are the top engineering institutes in India, and have a very high bar for entry.
But 3 Idiots was right, they are just hyped up pressure cookers. You get disillusioned with their charm pretty quickly.

Later in life, I applied to several graduate programs in the US, and was reasonably confident that I would get into one of these prestigious institutions for a doctorate
given my undergraduate and Master's were exceptionally productive and I did incredible work during those times.
But boy was I wrong.
I went a year with no acceptances.
Luckily I still ended up with an amazing advisor, and I love my research and academics.
I'm currently taking graduate level courses on quantum mechanics and condensed matter physics!

But I sometimes wonder if these supposedly prestigious institutions really are doing a good job retaining the best talent. And I have my reasons.
My group's recent works [[dynamarq](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03360)], [[DPQCs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23751)],
[[fastgap](/assets/pdfs/papers/fastgap.pdf)], [[SpecMatching](/assets/pdfs/papers/specmatching.pdf)],
either find serious issues in a lot of the earlier works, or surpass and improve them dramatically. 
A couple of the papers I'm dunking on aren't even peer reviewed and yet have many citations.
And all of these earlier works happen to be from top tier universities.

Here's an anecdote: this [[paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.07433)] seems to do a great job computing decoder soft output.
Yet their explanation of _why_ the complementary matching decode is slow seems unconvincing. They cite a personal communication.
I wouldn't be surprised if they don't understand it themselves: this could even be a case of a telephone-game-like garbling.

On the other hand, my works can certainly improve, and I do hope that they are peer reviewed by decent people who can override prestige bias.

Of course, I would be wrong to not take into account AI's help in my works. But I'm confident that I would have arrived at the same solutions and conclusions
that Claude did given more time. A LOT more time.
For example, in fastgap, my initial idea was to accelerate the complementary decode using graph compression, similar to what I did in SpecMatching.
I considered [[r-nets](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0409057)]
and construction of "super-nodes" that route augmenting paths with some error in the approximated metric.
The most significant of Claude's contribution was to use [[landmarks](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spr06/cos423/Handouts/GH05.pdf)].
The approximation error in which case is one-sided. Would I have found this paper by myself in a week or two? Maybe?

In my interactions with AI, I've found that the questions you ask it shape the trajectory of your research.
And so far, I've rarely seen Claude posing significant questions rather than doing what I asked it to. (I don't even understand why it listens to me instead of doing whatever it wants.)
Also, the distance preservation for yoking in fastgap was all Claude. It gave me the answer before I could even think about it lol! Saved me a lot of trouble.
And I would have never been able to implement all of this in C++ in a few hours. As an ICPC world finalist, I know a thing or two about that.

If someone like me (with AI) can do this, imagine what thousands of other researchers in the same boat can produce.
In a few years, I suspect the prestige that props up these institutions may end up disappearing entirely.
The very idea of prestige is cooked.
